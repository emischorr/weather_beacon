01Overview & Goal

What this thing is, and the one number that defines success.

This is a battery-powered indoor/outdoor environment sensor. On a fixed schedule (default 15 minutes) the nRF52840 wakes from deep sleep, takes a single forced-mode reading from the BME280, packs the values into a BTHome v2 service-data advertisement, transmits a brief burst on the three BLE advertising channels, and goes back to sleep. It never opens a connection — it is a pure beacon.

A separate OpenMQTTGateway node (ESP32 within BLE range) is permanently listening. It decodes the BTHome payload and republishes the readings to your MQTT broker, where Home Assistant or any other consumer can pick them up.

The goal, stated as a target

The success criterion is deliberately simple: device runtime must exceed the batteries' own shelf life. In other words, the sensor should sip so little that the cells age out (self-discharge / shelf rating) before the firmware can ever drain them. As the battery section shows, with two firmware tweaks this is met with enormous margin — the limiting factor stops being your design and becomes the chemistry on the label.

Design verdict

No board surgery needed. The two consequential power optimizations are pure firmware. Desoldering the tiny XIAO would add risk for zero practical benefit, because runtime is already capped by battery shelf life, not by current draw.

Why lithium, not your NiMH

Across a multi-year horizon, NiMH self-discharge becomes the dominant loss — you'd be recharging cells the firmware never came close to draining, which defeats "set and forget." NiMH also starts at only 2.4 V and sags toward the nRF's 1.7 V floor sooner. Lithium AAA holds a flat ~3.0 V, won't leak like alkaline, and its shelf rating is your runtime ceiling here. Keep the rechargeables for something that cycles often.

Power-path assumption

This reference assumes lithium AAA cells fed through the BAT pads and the onboard LDO. The regulator's ~1–2 µA quiescent draw is negligible here and, in exchange, it clamps fresh lithium (~3.6 V open-circuit) down to a safe 3.3 V. Switching to NiMH would change the design: you'd power the 3V3 pin directly, skip the LDO and the VBAT divider entirely, and read the battery from the chip's internal VDD channel instead.

Choosing the I²C pins

On the nRF52840 the I²C peripheral can be routed to any GPIO — the pins aren't fixed in hardware. The Arduino core defaults Wire to D4 (P0.04) / D5 (P0.05), which work with a bare Wire.begin(). Any other pair — like the D9 / D10 used here, chosen because they sit right beside 3V3 and GND for tidy wiring — must be declared first with Wire.setPins(SDA, SCL) before Wire.begin(). Both sensors ride the same Wire bus, so that one remap covers them together.

Safety — read before first flash

Remove the AAA cells before plugging in USB. The XIAO's onboard BQ25101 charger will try to charge whatever sits on the BAT pads when USB is present. Pushing charge current into primary lithium (or alkaline) cells risks venting, leakage, or rupture. Flash with batteries out; install them after.

Most BME280 breakouts include I²C pull-ups (4.7 k–10 k). If you use a bare chip, add pull-ups from SDA and SCL to 3V3. The BME280 stays on the 3V3 rail through sleep, drawing ~0.1 µA in standby — negligible, so there's no need to gate its power.

04Battery Estimation

Why the answer is "years and years" — and where the real ceiling sits.

The key insight

At a 10–15 minute reporting interval, the active work (sensor read + a few advertisement packets) is vanishingly small compared to the time spent asleep. The whole budget is therefore dominated by sleep current. Get that down and runtime balloons.

average current ≈ Isleep + (Qwake × wakes/day) / 86400 s
runtime ≈ usable capacity (mAh) / average current (mA)

Assumptions

  • Interval 15 min → 96 wakes/day (10 min → 144; the difference is < 1 year, so pick by data freshness, not battery).
  • Charge per wake ≈ 0.5 mA·s — a conservative ~300 ms advertising burst plus a forced-mode BME280 read. That works out to only ~0.5 µA of average contribution.
  • Usable capacity: lithium AAA ≈ 1200 mAh, NiMH AAA ≈ 800 mAh.
  • Sleep current per configuration is the variable that actually matters (below).

Sleep-current scenarios & resulting life

Firmware configurationSleep currentAvg current (15 min)Life · 2× Li AAA

"Capped ≈ 20 yr" = the Energizer Ultimate Lithium shelf rating becomes the limit; consumption alone would run far longer. NiMH (800 mAh) gives ~16 yr on consumption at 5.6 µA, but self-discharge realistically limits it to ~2–3 yr — which is why lithium wins.
What this means for your decisions

Stay on Arduino. Its achievable ~5 µA already pushes runtime past the battery's shelf life, so Zephyr's extra ~2 µA delivers no real-world gain. Don't desolder. Even the untuned ~20 µA case clears "several years." The only things you must do are two lines of firmware — covered next.

05Firmware — Getting Started

A complete Arduino starter sketch, plus the toolchain setup around it.

Toolchain

  • Board support: install the Adafruit nRF52 core, then select Seeed XIAO nRF52840 (the Bluefruit-based core, not the mbed core — Bluefruit gives the low-power advertising API used here).
  • Libraries (Library Manager): Adafruit BME280 Library, Adafruit Unified Sensor, Adafruit SPIFlash (for QSPI deep-power-down).
  • Upload: double-tap RESET to enter the UF2 bootloader if the port doesn't appear. Batteries out while USB is connected.

The two optimizations that matter

  1. QSPI flash → deep power-down (flashTransport.runCommand(0xB9)). This alone takes you from ~20 µA toward ~5 µA.
  2. Keep the VBAT divider disconnected (drive VBAT_ENABLE HIGH; only pull it LOW for the few ms you read battery voltage). Saves the divider's ~2.3 µA.
xiao_bme280_bthome.ino
Notes & calibration

The pin macros PIN_VBAT, VBAT_ENABLE, and the LED names are defined by the Seeed XIAO variant in the Adafruit core; if a name doesn't resolve, check your installed core version. The battery divider ratio (VBAT_DIVIDER) and reference should be checked against a multimeter once — the value is a good starting point, not gospel. Battery percent from lithium/NiMH is approximate (flat discharge curve), so the raw voltage object (0x0C) is the more honest reading in Home Assistant.

06BTHome v2 Payload

Exactly what goes on the air — service UUID 0xFCD2, unencrypted.

The advertisement carries one Service-Data field (AD type 0x16) with 16-bit UUID 0xFCD2. The first byte (0x40) flags BTHome v2, unencrypted, non-trigger. Then come measurement objects in ascending ID order, each value little-endian.

Object IDMeasurementTypeFactor / Unit
0x01Batteryuint81 · %
0x02Temperaturesint160.01 · °C
0x03Humidityuint160.01 · %
0x04Pressureuint240.01 · hPa
0x0CVoltageuint160.001 · V
Total advertisement ≈ 23 bytes incl. flags — comfortably under the 31-byte legacy advertising limit.

07Commissioning & OpenMQTTGateway

From first power-up to data in your broker.

  1. Flash the sketch (batteries removed). Open the Serial Monitor only if you added debug prints — leave them out of the deployed build to keep wake time short.
  2. Insert the lithium AAAs. The device begins its wake/advertise/sleep cycle immediately.
  3. OpenMQTTGateway with the BLE/BTHome decoder enabled will see the 0xFCD2 beacon and publish to a topic such as home/OMG/BTtoMQTT/<MAC>. No pairing or whitelist is needed for an unencrypted BTHome beacon.
  4. Home Assistant picks up the readings via the MQTT integration (and can auto-discover BTHome through OMG). Confirm temperature, humidity, pressure, battery % and voltage all arrive.
  5. Range check: keep the sensor within solid BLE range of the gateway. If it's marginal, raise TX_POWER_DBM a step — the energy cost is still negligible at this duty cycle.

Optimization checklist (firmware-only — no hardware risk)

  • ☐ QSPI flash deep-power-down sent in setup()
  • ☐ VBAT_ENABLE held HIGH except during the battery read
  • ☐ All three onboard LEDs driven HIGH (off)
  • ☐ BME280 in forced mode (sleeps between reads)
  • ☐ Advertising burst short (≈ 300 ms) then explicitly stopped
  • ☐ No Serial / debug prints in the deployed build

08Upgrade A — Ambient Light Optional · BH1750

Rough sunshine tracking that piggybacks on the existing I²C bus.

The BH1750 is a digital I²C lux sensor. It hangs off the same SDA/SCL lines as the BME280 (different address — no conflict), runs on the 3.3 V rail, and in one-shot mode powers itself down to ~0.01 µA between reads. Electrically it's a clean fit: two extra wires onto the shared bus, no new constraints.

Compatibility — clean fit

The sensor respects every design assumption. It shares the I²C bus, sips ~0.01 µA between reads, and adds no measurable load — the device keeps its clean 15-minute-only wake cadence. You report instantaneous lux each cycle and let your broker-side software threshold and sum it into sun-hours. The one thing to get right is the lux threshold that defines "sunny" — covered below.

Defining "sunny" — the lux threshold

This is the real accuracy knob — it matters more than your sampling interval. The meteorological benchmark (the WMO standard behind official "sunshine hours") counts sunshine when the direct solar beam exceeds 120 W/m², measured perpendicular to the sun with a pyrheliometer. A flat BH1750 can't reproduce that: it reads global horizontal illuminance in lux — direct sun plus diffuse skylight — so it is inherently a proxy. The job is to pick a lux value that best separates "direct sun on the bed" from "bright cloud," then calibrate it.

Typical horizontal illuminance, for reference:

Sky conditionApprox. illuminance
Direct sun (full daylight + beam)32,000 – 100,000+ lux
Bright; sun behind thin cloud / open shade under clear sky10,000 – 25,000 lux
Typical overcast, midday1,000 – 10,000 lux
Heavy / dark overcast< 1,000 lux

A sensible starting threshold is ~30,000 lux — it sits in the gap between bright overcast (~25 k) and direct sun (~32 k). Raise it toward 40–50 k if bright cloud gets falsely counted as sun; lower it toward ~20 k if low-angle morning, evening, or winter sun is being missed. In an Option-A build the threshold lives in your broker-side summing software (the device just ships raw lux), so you can re-tune it against recorded data without ever reflashing.

Germany / high-latitude caveat

Because the sensor lies flat, absolute lux scales with sun elevation — the same "sun is out" condition reads far brighter at summer noon than on a low winter morning. For a garden bed that's fine: the growing-season sun (spring–autumn, higher arc) is what decides "full vs part sun," and ~30 k captures it well. Deep-winter low sun may be under-counted, which doesn't change the seasonal verdict. Spend ten minutes calibrating against two or three hand-observed days for your exact spot and orientation.

BH1750 saturation — keep the threshold below it

In its standard high-resolution mode the BH1750 tops out near ~54,000 lux (a 16-bit register, ~65,535 raw ÷ 1.2). Bright summer direct sun (70,000–100,000 lux) will saturate it. For a boolean "is it sunny?" test that's harmless — a clipped reading just means "well above threshold" — but it carries two consequences: keep the threshold comfortably under ~50 k so it stays reachable, and don't trust the absolute lux number in full sun. If you do want true lux in full sun, shorten the sensor's integration time (MTreg) to push the ceiling past ~100 k, trading away some low-light resolution.

09Upgrade B — Soil Moisture Optional · Capacitive

Feasible — but only if you power-gate it. Wired naively, it breaks the project.

Design conflict — read this first

The common capacitive sensors are built around a 555 oscillator that draws ~5 mA continuously whenever powered. Tied straight to 3V3 that's ~1000× your sleep budget — the cells would be flat in roughly a week, breaking the core requirement outright. The fix: power the sensor from a GPIO (or a small MOSFET / load switch) and energize it only for the ~300 ms a reading takes. Gated that way it adds ~1.5 mA·s per cycle (~1.7 µA average) and the multi-year, shelf-life-capped runtime survives intact.

This sensor is analog, so it also costs one ADC pin plus a calibration step (dry-air vs. fully-submerged raw values). Choose a sensor rated for 3.3 V operation — several on the market are designed for 5 V and read poorly on our rail.

Calibration & accuracy

Capacitive output drifts slightly with supply voltage. While the LDO holds 3.3 V (most of the battery's life) readings are stable; near end-of-life, as the rail follows the cells down, expect some drift. For best results read ratiometrically (ADC reference = VDD) or sample VDD alongside and normalize. For "is the plant thirsty" purposes, a fixed reference plus a one-time dry/wet calibration is plenty.

Mixing upgrades → a fourth build

The two upgrades don't collide: light lives on I²C (D9/D10), soil on an analog pin (A0) plus a power pin (D1). All three variants — base, +light, +soil — share one firmware base, so you can also fit both at once for a fully-loaded node.
